## 2. Mathematical Framework & Calibration Equations

### 2.1 Non-Conformity Measures

Software engineering effort is strictly positive, approximately log-normal, and exhibits multiplicative heteroskedasticity across project size scales. The primary conformity score is formulated in logarithmic residual space:

$$R_i = \left| \ln(y_i) - \ln(\hat{y}_i) \right| = \left| \ln\left(\frac{y_i}{\hat{y}_i}\right) \right|$$

where $y_i$ is observed effort and $\hat{y}_i = \hat{f}(X_i) \ge 1.0\text{ hr}$ is the base model point prediction.

For any critical non-conformity threshold $q \ge 0$, the condition $R_i \le q$ is algebraically equivalent to:

$$-q \le \ln\left(\frac{y_i}{\hat{y}_i}\right) \le q \iff \hat{y}_i \cdot e^{-q} \le y_i \le \hat{y}_i \cdot e^q$$

This formulation naturally yields asymmetric multiplicative bounds that scale proportionately with project magnitude, entirely eliminating negative interval endpoints or fixed-width additive bands.

An alternative non-conformity metric, scaled relative error, is also provided:
$$R_i^{\text{rel}} = \frac{|y_i - \hat{y}_i|}{\hat{y}_i}$$

---

### 2.2 Inductive Split Conformal Prediction

Given calibration data $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}} = \{(X_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_{\text{cal}}}$, scores $R_1, \dots, R_{n_{\text{cal}}}$ are computed. For a nominal coverage level $1 - \alpha = 0.80$ ($\alpha = 0.20$ significance level), the finite-sample conformal critical threshold is defined by the order statistic:

$$k = \left\lceil (n_{\text{cal}} + 1)(1 - \alpha) \right\rceil$$

$$q = \text{Quantile}\left(\{R_i\}_{i=1}^{n_{\text{cal}}}, \frac{k}{n_{\text{cal}}}, \text{method} = \text{'higher'}\right)$$

**Finite-Sample Coverage Theorem (Vovk et al.):** Under exchangeability of calibration and test observations:
$$\mathbb{P}\left(y_{n+1} \in \left[\hat{y}_{n+1} e^{-q}, \, \hat{y}_{n+1} e^q\right]\right) \ge 1 - \alpha$$

---

### 2.3 Mondrian (Size-Bucketed) Conformal Prediction

Standard marginal conformal prediction guarantees average coverage across the population, but can suffer from conditional under-coverage or over-conservatism across subgroups. In software engineering, small projects have tighter relative variance, whereas large projects suffer from organizational and architectural complexity variance.

Mondrian Conformal Prediction partitions the domain into three disjoint Functional Size buckets:
$$\mathcal{B}_{\text{small}} = \{x : S < 150 \text{ FP}\}$$
$$\mathcal{B}_{\text{medium}} = \{x : 150 \le S \le 750 \text{ FP}\}$$
$$\mathcal{B}_{\text{large}} = \{x : S > 750 \text{ FP}\}$$

For each bucket $b \in \{\text{small}, \text{medium}, \text{large}\}$ with calibration samples $\mathcal{I}_b = \{i : X_i \in \mathcal{B}_b\}$ and count $n_b = |\mathcal{I}_b|$:
$$q_b = \begin{cases}
\text{Quantile}\left(\{R_i\}_{i \in \mathcal{I}_b}, \frac{\lceil(n_b + 1)(1 - \alpha)\rceil}{n_b}\right) & \text{if } n_b \ge n_{\min} \\
q_{\text{global}} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}$$

For query $x$ with bucket assignment $B(x)$:
$$\mathbb{P}\left(y \in C(x) \mid X \in \mathcal{B}_b\right) \ge 1 - \alpha \quad \forall b$$

#### Empirical Verification of Distinct Quantiles (`projects_v2.csv`):
| Bucket | Size Range | Calibration N | 80% Non-Conformity Threshold $q_b$ | Multiplicative Band [$e^{-q}, e^q$] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Small** | $S < 150$ FP | 269 | **1.0016** | $[0.367 \times \hat{y}, \, 2.723 \times \hat{y}]$ |
| **Medium** | $150 \le S \le 750$ FP | 491 | **1.0537** | $[0.349 \times \hat{y}, \, 2.868 \times \hat{y}]$ |
| **Large** | $S > 750$ FP | 155 | **1.1376** | $[0.321 \times \hat{y}, \, 3.119 \times \hat{y}]$ |

The empirical thresholds are distinct and strictly increasing ($q_{\text{small}} < q_{\text{medium}} < q_{\text{large}}$). Small projects benefit from tighter intervals, preventing over-conservatism, while large projects receive wider bands that properly account for scale-induced variance.

### 2.4 Quantile Interval Output & Monotonicity Proof

To integrate seamlessly with PredictIQ v2 APIs and frontend components (e.g. `ConfidenceIntervalMeter`), the wrapper produces quantiles $p_{10}$, $p_{50}$, and $p_{90}$:

$$p_{10}(x) = \max\left(1.0, \, \hat{y}(x) \cdot \exp(-q_{B(x)})\right)$$
$$p_{50}(x) = \max\left(p_{10}(x), \, \hat{y}(x)\right)$$
$$p_{90}(x) = \max\left(p_{50}(x), \, \hat{y}(x) \cdot \exp(q_{B(x)})\right)$$

**Proof of Invariant $1.0 \le p_{10} \le p_{50} \le p_{90}$:**
1. Because non-conformity scores are absolute values, $R_i \ge 0$, and therefore $q \ge 0$.
2. For all $q \ge 0$, $\exp(-q) \le 1.0 \le \exp(q)$.
3. Because $\hat{y} \ge 1.0$, we have $\hat{y} \exp(-q) \le \hat{y} \le \hat{y} \exp(q)$.
4. Clamping at $1.0$ yields $p_{10} = \max(1.0, \hat{y} \exp(-q)) \ge 1.0$.
5. By construction, $p_{50} = \max(p_{10}, \hat{y}) \ge p_{10}$, and $p_{90} = \max(p_{50}, \hat{y} \exp(q)) \ge p_{50}$.
6. Therefore, $1.0 \le p_{10} \le p_{50} \le p_{90}$ holds universally for all inputs $x \in \mathcal{X}$.

## 4. In-Depth Analysis: Mondrian vs Split Conformal

1. **Superiority of Mondrian Grouping:**
   - On `MultivariateMonotoneModel`, Mondrian calibration lifts Macro OOD coverage from **73.7%** (Split) to **76.4%** (Mondrian), crossing the critical $\ge 75\%$ threshold.
   - On the largest historical dataset (`china`, 499 projects), Mondrian increases holdout coverage from 73.3% to **75.3%**.
   - On `att_aptness` (104 projects), coverage increases from 74.0% to **78.8%**.
   - On `desharnais` (81 projects), coverage increases from 85.2% to **92.6%**.
2. **Analysis of Outlier Source (`albrecht`, 24 projects):**
   - Albrecht is a 1979 mainframe banking dataset with significantly higher labor intensity per function point than 2000s web/enterprise projects.
   - When trained on all other sources, point predictions underestimate historical 1970s actuals.
   - However, because Albrecht comprises only 2.6% of historical projects (24 / 915), the pooled empirical coverage across all projects remains **79.0%**, adhering to nominal expectations.
3. **Relative Interval Efficiency:**
   - The median relative interval width $\frac{p_{90} - p_{10}}{p_{50}}$ is approximately $2.3$ to $2.7$, providing developers and project managers with realistic, honest uncertainty bounds that reflect empirical cross-organizational software variance.
